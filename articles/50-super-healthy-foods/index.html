A List of 50 Super Healthy Foods

By Youssef EL MONTASSIR · Published 25 Nov 2026 · 13 min read

Healthy foods do not have to be exotic, expensive or complicated. The best ones are everyday ingredients, such as fruit, vegetables, beans, nuts, whole grains and fish, that give your body fibre, vitamins, minerals and quality protein for relatively few calories. This guide lists 50 healthy foods, grouped into nine food groups, with a short note on why each one earns its place and an easy way to eat it.

Healthy foods: What makes them nutritious?

Healthy foods are nutrient-dense: they deliver a lot of nutrition for the energy they contain. Most are minimally processed, which means they keep their natural fibre and nutrients and have little added sugar, salt or unhealthy fat.

The World Health Organization describes a healthy diet as one built on a variety of minimally processed foods, and puts vegetables, fruit, legumes, nuts and whole grains at its core. It also advises limiting free sugars, salt and saturated and trans fats.

No single food can do this job alone. What protects your health over time is the overall pattern of what you eat, which is why this list covers nine food groups rather than a handful of "superfoods".

How to choose healthy foods for a balanced diet

A simple rule helps: build your plate from several groups instead of relying on one. Harvard's Healthy Eating Plate suggests filling about half the plate with vegetables and fruit, about a quarter with whole grains and about a quarter with healthy protein, and using healthy oils for cooking.

A few habits make choosing easier:

Aim for variety and colour. Different colours usually mean different nutrients. Reach for whole foods first. Choose the apple over the juice and the brown rice over the white. Count your fruit and vegetables. WHO recommends at least 400 g (about five portions) a day for adults, not counting starchy roots such as potatoes. Read labels on packaged foods. Watch for added sugar and salt, even on products that look healthy.

no added sugar or excess salt.

You do not need to eat all 50 foods below. Treat the list as a menu: pick a few from each group, rotate them through the week, and adjust for your tastes, budget, allergies and health needs.

50 healthy foods to add variety to your meals

Each food below comes with what it offers and a simple way to eat it. None of them is a miracle food: they work best as part of a varied diet.

Fruits and berries

Fruit brings fibre, vitamins and natural sweetness. Whole fruit is a better choice than juice because you keep the fibre.

Apples

Apples are a good source of fibre, including pectin, and they keep well and travel easily. Eat them with the skin on, sliced into oatmeal or paired with a spoonful of nut butter.

Bananas

Bananas provide potassium and quick, easy-to-digest energy. Slice one over porridge, or eat it before or after exercise.

Blueberries

Blueberries are rich in plant compounds called anthocyanins, which give them their deep colour, and they are relatively low in sugar for a fruit. Add them to yogurt, or use frozen ones in smoothies.

Oranges

Oranges are best known for vitamin C and also give you fibre and folate. Eat them as a snack or add segments to a salad.

Strawberries

Strawberries are high in vitamin C and low in calories. Slice them into a spinach salad, or serve them with plain yogurt.

Kiwi

Kiwi is another strong source of vitamin C and provides fibre and vitamin K. Cut it in half and scoop it out with a spoon, or add slices to a fruit bowl.

Pears

Pears are among the higher-fibre fruits, especially when eaten with the skin. Enjoy them

Mango

Mango provides vitamin C and vitamin A (as beta-carotene). It is sweeter than most fruit, so a small portion goes a long way: try it diced in salsa or blended in a smoothie.

Vegetables

Vegetables are low in calories and high in fibre, vitamins and minerals. Eating a range of colours is the easiest way to cover your nutrient bases.

Spinach

Spinach provides folate, vitamin K and iron, and it is quick to cook. Wilt it into soups, eggs and pasta, or use it raw as a salad base.

Broccoli

Broccoli is a good source of vitamin C, vitamin K and fibre. Steam or roast it lightly so it stays bright green and slightly crisp.

Carrots

Carrots are rich in beta-carotene, which your body turns into vitamin A. Eat them raw with hummus, grate them into salads, or roast them with olive oil.

Bell peppers

Bell peppers, especially the red ones, are packed with vitamin C. Slice them as a crunchy snack, stuff them with grains and beans, or add them to stir-fries.

Cauliflower

Cauliflower is a versatile, fibre-rich vegetable from the same family as broccoli. Roast it in florets, or pulse it into a rice-style side dish.

Tomatoes

Tomatoes contain vitamin C, potassium and the antioxidant lycopene, which is easier to absorb from cooked tomatoes. Use them fresh in salads or simmered into sauces and stews.

Kale

Kale is a leafy green with vitamins K, A and C and a good amount of fibre. Massage it with a little lemon and oil for salads, or add it to soups.

Zucchini

Zucchini is mild, light and high in water, so it suits almost any dish. Grill it, spiralise it into noodles, or bake it into savoury fritters.

Mushrooms

Mushrooms are low in calories and provide B vitamins and selenium. Sauté them with garlic, add them to omelettes, or use them to bulk out meat dishes.

Legumes

Legumes (also called pulses) combine plant protein with fibre, and they are budget- friendly and long-lasting. WHO lists them among the core foods of a healthy diet.

Lentils

Lentils are rich in protein, fibre, iron and folate, and they cook quickly without soaking. Simmer them into soups and stews, or toss cooked lentils into salads.

Chickpeas

Chickpeas provide protein, fibre and minerals such as iron and magnesium. Roast them for a crunchy snack, blend them into hummus, or add them to curries.

Black beans

Black beans offer fibre, protein and folate. Use them in burritos, soups and grain bowls, or mash them into veggie burgers.

Kidney beans

Kidney beans are high in fibre and plant protein. Add them to chilli, salads and rice dishes. Dried kidney beans must be soaked and boiled thoroughly before eating, and canned ones are already cooked.

Edamame

Edamame are young soybeans and a complete plant protein, with fibre and folate. Steam them in the pod with a pinch of salt, or add the shelled beans to salads and noodle dishes.

Protein-rich foods

Protein supports muscle, repair and fullness. Harvard's Healthy Eating Plate points to fish, poultry, beans and nuts as the better protein choices.

Eggs

Eggs provide high-quality protein plus vitamin B12, choline and vitamin D. Boil them for a portable snack, scramble them with vegetables, or poach them over whole-grain toast.

Salmon

Salmon is an oily fish rich in omega-3 fatty acids, protein and vitamin D. Bake or pan-sear a fillet, or flake canned salmon into salads and sandwiches.

Sardines

Sardines are small oily fish with omega-3s, calcium (when the soft bones are eaten) and vitamin B12. Eat them on whole-grain toast or mix them through pasta with lemon and herbs.

Chicken breast

Chicken breast is a lean source of protein that fits many cuisines. Grill, bake or poach it, then slice it over salads and grain bowls, and avoid heavily processed versions such as nuggets.

Tofu

Tofu is made from soybeans and provides plant protein, and calcium when it is set with calcium salts. It takes on the flavour of whatever it is cooked with, so try it in stir-fries, curries and scrambles.

Tempeh

Tempeh is fermented soybeans pressed into a firm cake, with protein, fibre and a nutty flavour. Slice and pan-fry it, or crumble it into chilli and tacos.

Nuts and seeds

Nuts and seeds are small but nutrient-dense, with unsaturated fats, protein, fibre and minerals. They are also energy-dense, so a small handful (about 30 g) is a sensible portion.

Almonds

Almonds are a good source of vitamin E, magnesium and fibre. Eat them as a snack, chop them over porridge, or choose unsalted, unsweetened versions.

Walnuts

Walnuts stand out for their plant-based omega-3 (alpha-linolenic acid). Sprinkle them over salads and yogurt, or bake them into wholemeal bread.

Pistachios

Pistachios offer protein, fibre and potassium, and shelling them slows you down, which helps with portion control. Eat them plain, or crush them over roasted vegetables and grains.

Chia seeds

Chia seeds are rich in fibre and omega-3 fats, and they swell into a gel in liquid. Stir them into yogurt or oats overnight, or make a simple chia pudding with milk and fruit.

Flaxseeds

Flaxseeds provide fibre and plant omega-3s, but they are best eaten ground, because whole seeds often pass through undigested. Mix ground flaxseed into smoothies, porridge or batter.

Pumpkin seeds

Pumpkin seeds are a good source of magnesium, zinc and iron. Toast them lightly as a snack, or scatter them over soups and salads.

Whole grains

Whole grains keep the bran and germ, so they supply more fibre and nutrients than refined grains. Harvard notes they also have a milder effect on blood sugar and insulin than white bread and white rice.

Oats

Oats contain beta-glucan, a soluble fibre that helps you feel full. Cook them as porridge, soak them overnight, or blend them into baking.

Quinoa

Quinoa is a seed that is cooked like a grain, and it provides protein, fibre and minerals such as magnesium. Use it as a base for salads and bowls, or as a rice replacement.

Brown rice

Brown rice keeps its bran layer, so it offers more fibre than white rice. Serve it with beans and vegetables, or use it in stir-fries and stuffed vegetables.

Barley

Barley is a chewy grain high in fibre, including beta-glucan. Add it to soups and stews, or cook it as a hearty side like rice. Choose hulled barley over pearl barley for more fibre.

Whole-grain bread

Whole-grain bread gives you more fibre than white bread, but check the label: "whole grain" or "whole wheat" should be the first ingredient. Use it for sandwiches or toast with eggs, avocado or hummus.

Dairy and fortified alternatives

Dairy foods provide calcium, protein and often vitamin B12 and iodine. If you avoid dairy, choose unsweetened alternatives that are fortified with calcium.

Plain yogurt

Plain yogurt gives you protein, calcium and, in many cases, live cultures. Choose unsweetened versions and add your own fruit, nuts or seeds instead of buying flavoured, sugary ones.

Kefir

Kefir is a fermented milk drink with live cultures, protein and calcium, and a tangy taste. Drink it plain, or blend it into smoothies and dressings.

Milk

Milk is a convenient source of calcium, protein and several B vitamins. Use it in porridge, coffee and smoothies. Plain milk contains natural sugars (lactose), which are not counted as added sugars.

Fortified unsweetened soy drink

Soy drink is the plant-based milk closest to cow's milk in protein, and fortified versions add calcium and often vitamin B12 and vitamin D. Check that the label says "unsweetened" and "fortified", and use it as you would milk.

Healthy fats

Fats are essential, and WHO advises favouring unsaturated fats over saturated and trans fats. These three foods are rich in unsaturated fats and easy to use in everyday cooking.

Extra-virgin olive oil

Extra-virgin olive oil is high in monounsaturated fat and plant compounds called polyphenols. Use it for dressings, drizzling over cooked dishes and gentle cooking.

Tahini

Tahini is a paste of ground sesame seeds, with unsaturated fats, calcium and minerals. Stir it into hummus and dressings, or drizzle it over roasted vegetables.

Olives

Olives provide monounsaturated fat and vitamin E. They are naturally salty from curing, so enjoy them in moderation: add a few to salads, grain dishes and tapenade.

Starchy vegetables

Starchy vegetables supply energy along with fibre, potassium and vitamins. They are filling and nutritious, but they act more like carbohydrate foods than like leafy greens, so balance them with other vegetables. WHO does not count potatoes and sweet potatoes towards its 400 g daily fruit-and-vegetable target, and Harvard keeps potatoes off the vegetable portion of its plate because of their effect on blood sugar.

Potatoes

Potatoes provide potassium, vitamin C and fibre, especially with the skin on. Boil, bake or roast them rather than deep-frying, and let them cool for a potato salad.

Sweet potatoes

Sweet potatoes are rich in beta-carotene (vitamin A) and fibre. Bake them whole, roast them in wedges, or mash them with a little olive oil.

Pumpkin

Pumpkin is a lower-calorie starchy vegetable with beta-carotene and potassium. Roast it, or blend it into a soup or a curry.

Corn

Corn provides fibre and some B vitamins, and it counts as a whole grain when eaten whole, for example as corn on the cob or popcorn without added butter and sugar. Add kernels to salads, soups and tacos.

How to include healthy foods in your everyday meals

You do not need a new diet to eat more healthy foods. Small swaps and repeatable habits are enough.

Start with breakfast. Oats with berries and nuts, or eggs with spinach on whole-grain toast, cover several food groups before 9 a.m. Fill half your plate with vegetables and fruit. This follows Harvard's Healthy Eating Plate and is an easy visual rule. Swap refined for whole. Choose brown rice, whole-grain bread or quinoa instead of white versions. Make beans and lentils a regular dinner. Add them to soups, salads and curries a few times a week. Keep healthy snacks in sight. A bowl of fruit, a handful of nuts or carrots with hummus beats reaching for packaged snacks. Eat fish regularly. Salmon and sardines are quick options, and canned fish works well. Cook with olive oil. Use it in place of butter where you can. Batch-cook and freeze. Soups, grain salads and roasted vegetables keep well, and frozen produce without added sugar or salt is a good backup. Limit highly processed foods. The 2025-2030 US Dietary Guidelines also advise cutting back on foods high in added sugar, salt and refined carbohydrates.

As a simple one-day example: porridge with blueberries and walnuts, a lentil soup with salad for lunch, an apple and yogurt as a snack, and baked salmon with broccoli and quinoa for dinner.

Final Thoughts

Healthy foods are the ordinary, minimally processed ones: fruit, vegetables, legumes, whole grains, nuts, fish, eggs and dairy or fortified alternatives. Balance and variety do more for your health than any single "super" food, so start small. Pick three or four foods from this list that you have not eaten recently and add them to this week's shopping list.

Frequently asked questions

What are the healthiest foods to eat every day?

There is no single answer, but a good daily base is vegetables and fruit (at least 400 g, per WHO), whole grains, a source of healthy protein such as beans, fish, eggs or nuts, and a little healthy fat such as olive oil. Variety across these groups matters more than any one item.

What are the most nutritious foods?

Nutrient-dense foods give you the most vitamins, minerals and fibre per calorie. Leafy greens, berries, legumes, oily fish, eggs, nuts and whole grains all fit that description. No single food covers every nutrient, so the most nutritious diet is a varied one.

Are frozen fruits and vegetables healthy?

Yes. WHO says frozen and canned options are good choices as long as they have no added sugar or excess salt. They are often cheaper, last longer and reduce food waste. Check the label for sauces, syrups and added salt.

Do I need to eat all 50 healthy foods?

No. The list is a menu, not a checklist. Choose foods you enjoy from each group, rotate them across the week, and adapt for allergies, medical conditions and your budget. If you have a health condition, ask a doctor or registered dietitian for personal advice.
